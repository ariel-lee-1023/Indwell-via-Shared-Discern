# How Learning Works: seven principles that change teaching choices

**Sections**: 7 | **Depth**: study
Susan A. Ambrose, Michael W. Bridges, Michele DiPietro, Marsha C. Lovett, and Marie K. Norman, 2010. Based on the supplied Markdown and PDF. Here p means printed page; the PDF file page is usually p+26. The seven chapters move from teaching dilemmas to research explanations and practical strategies. An illustrative classroom case is not evidence that each strategy has its own randomized trial.

## Mental Model

Learning depends on what learners bring, how knowledge is organized and used, the goals and environment shaping effort, practice and feedback, and regulation of learning. Similar observable errors may require different support. The seven principles organize conditional judgments, not seven mandatory stages in an AI conversation.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

### Prior knowledge and organization

Available but inactive knowledge calls for different support than missing background. A learner who knows “permission” but treats it as a guarantee of factual accuracy needs its object and scope distinguished; more permission terminology may reinforce the wrong link. A counterexample alone may not change an everyday misconception if it is dismissed as exceptional. Expose the explanatory difference and observe whether the earlier relationship persists in later situations.

More complex organization is not always better. Chapter 2 uses material such as kinship relations to show that knowledge can be connected differently for different tasks. Expert organization is often richer, organized by deeper features, and matched to use. Show a useful organization without assuming the learner already possesses the teacher's framework. Locations: p47/PDF73 and p65/PDF91.

**Skill example:** to explain why errors can remain after checks pass, distinguish checks on format, permission, and factual accuracy. A single unqualified “quality improves” arrow is less targeted. Whether an arrow is causal must still be checked against the source; this book does not establish the example's mechanism.

### Motivation and climate

Chapter 3 relates motivation to goals, value, and expectancy; learners may have multiple goals that differ from the teacher's. Appropriate choice can support control, while clear criteria and feasible support may strengthen expectancy of success (p70–71, p89). More freedom is not automatically better; choice costs can be considered alongside the handbook's learner-control research.

Chapter 6 treats intellectual, social, and emotional development together with course climate. Its continuum from explicitly marginalizing through implicitly marginalizing and implicitly centralizing to explicitly centralizing environments directs attention to climate, not diagnosis of a person or group from sparse chat data. An AI adaptation is to address disagreement seriously and avoid humiliation or unsupported diagnosis; this does not establish classroom belonging or developmental effects. Locations: p172 and p187.

### Mastery is more than one component

Figure 4.1 (p96/PDF122, visually checked) relates component skills, integration, and knowing when to apply learning. Experts may lose awareness of smaller decisions, producing expert blind spots. Decomposition can expose omissions, but isolated practice does not automatically yield fluent integration or transfer.

- If one recurrent component error obstructs the whole, demonstrate or briefly practise it, then return to the whole.
- If components work separately but coordination fails, reduce simultaneous demands and support integrated practice.
- If familiar problems succeed but method selection fails in new situations, compare applicability rather than add identical calculations.
- If success follows a cue, record the cue's role and later observe independent activation; do not record cued success as independent transfer.

Chapter 4 discusses conditional application at p107 and the need to uncover expert performance at p120. Combined with 4C/ID, these judgments can guide targeted practice within whole tasks; the seven principles are not components of 4C/ID.

## Worked Example

Chapter 5 emphasizes clear goals, appropriate challenge, sufficient practice quantity and frequency, and feedback that relates performance to goals and improvement. Provide information when it can be used; immediate and delayed feedback have no context-free ranking. Too many details may be unusable for novices. Prioritize consequential issues and provide a revision opportunity (p141–142, p152).

**Book dilemma:** different writing assignments without sustained practice of the same skill may not improve performance merely because final drafts receive extensive comments. The case distinguishes receiving feedback from using it in practice, not proof that all cross-genre writing instruction fails. See Chapter 5's opening dilemma and feedback discussion.

**Original skill feedback:** “You preserved the AND condition, but this arrow suggests access permission guarantees factual accuracy. Change it to ‘begin generation after both checks pass’ and place human review afterwards.” This gives an actionable criterion rather than “good understanding, but not deep enough.” Do not invent such a diagnosis without work to inspect.

## Self-direction and optional tools

Chapter 7 summarizes assessing the task, appraising strengths and weaknesses, planning, monitoring, and reflecting, noting that metacognitive skills do not inevitably develop on their own (p216/PDF242). AI can demonstrate choosing checkpoints and appropriately return judgment to the learner. Writing the learner's reflection for them does not establish their metacognition.

Appendices A–H cover self-assessment, concept maps, rubrics, learning objectives, ground rules, exam wrappers, checklists, and reader response/peer review. These are optional tools, not entry requirements. Appendix F was checked further (PDF278): connect preparation strategies, error patterns, and future adjustments rather than ask only about scores. Other appendices remain retrieval pointers; their complete forms were not reconstructed. Text extraction of a concept-map figure does not justify reproducing its precise layout.

## Key Takeaways

The seven principles synthesize varied research for teaching; they are not seven independently guaranteed interventions. The primary context is higher education. Transfer to children, professional practice, other cultures, and one-to-one AI requires checking conditions. Agreement, a single paraphrase, or one operation does not establish durable learning. Report observed performance and remaining uncertainty rather than certify mastery.
