## Mental Model

4C/ID addresses complex performance requiring integrated knowledge, skills, and attitudes, with transfer as a concern. It is not ten steps for every explanation or an arbitrary division of a task into ten pieces.

**Authors' mechanism account:** support schema construction and reasoning for nonrecurrent aspects, and rule formation followed by strengthening for recurrent aspects. Classification depends on the task, goal, and existing competence, not an action's appearance alone. Training separated fragments can miss coordination between them; whole tasks belong at the centre of design. Locations: Chapter 2, PDF31–33, 42; Chapter 3, PDF61–63.

## Frameworks & Structure

Chapters 1–3 introduce the approach, four components, and ten design activities. Chapters 4–13 address those activities. Chapters 14–16 cover domain-general skills, educational programs and assessment, and closing perspectives. Appendices and a glossary provide further tools.

### Four components

| Component | When it applies | Design action and adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| Learning tasks | Integrated performance of authentic or closely related tasks | Use varied whole tasks in simple-to-complex task classes; fade support within each class and restore it when complexity increases |
| Supportive information | Understanding, reasoning, and solving nonrecurrent aspects | Provide domain models, problem-solving methods, and cognitive feedback associated with a task class; keep them available rather than force all background into the moment of action |
| Procedural information | Recurrent aspects requiring how/when rules and prerequisites | Provide information just in time where possible, combine it with corrective feedback, and fade it; do not turn full theoretical accounts into step-by-step prompts |
| Part-task practice | A recurrent component requires high automaticity but receives insufficient repetition in whole tasks | Add practice after the whole task has established its meaning; neither drill every detail nor demand mastery of all parts before encountering the whole |

## Decision Rules & Judgment

**Attribution boundary:** source concepts and reported findings are paraphrased, not quotations. Recommendations addressed to this AI skill, hypothetical learner signals, accessibility safeguards, and delivery choices are proposed designs unless explicitly identified as a book method or case. They are not independently validated effects.


**Strategies versus rules.** A systematic approach to problem solving (SAP) offers phases and heuristics rather than an algorithm guaranteeing a solution. Cognitive rules concern actions under specified conditions. Determine which difficulty is present before offering reasoned demonstration, strategic support, or a short procedural instruction. Reducing every expert action to mechanical IF–THEN statements removes judgment. See Chapters 8–12.

**Ways of providing supportive information.** Chapter 7 discusses inductive/deductive and expository/inquisitory arrangements; it does not simply reject inquiry. Prior knowledge, time, and task matter, and inquiry may require more time and support (PDF186, 198). This can be coordinated with dialogic openness, without treating more open instruction as inherently superior.

**Simplifying whole tasks versus splitting tasks.** Prefer reducing conditions' complexity while retaining the whole—for example, a version with fewer constraints and lower risk. When a workable simple whole cannot be obtained, consider part-task sequencing. Forward or backward chaining with accumulation must still preserve coordination. This neither prohibits component practice nor requires beginners to perform every complex skill at once. See Chapter 6, PDF141, 155.

**First- and second-order support.** Support may address task execution and the learner's selection of subsequent tasks or appraisal of learning needs. Fading task-selection support requires evidence. Freedom to choose difficulty does not automatically produce an appropriate learning path. See Chapter 6 and Chapter 14, PDF315.

## Assessment and transfer

Chapter 5 specifies observable performance, conditions, and standards. In the model, performance standards remain fixed while task complexity and support change; the number of standards relevant to a particular task can differ (PDF132, printed p117). Chapter 15 discusses assessment programs and Miller's levels: knows, knows how, shows how, and does (PDF337, 339). An explanation is not actual execution; success in a simulation is not automatically workplace competence.

Assessment requires sampling across tasks. Do not turn a context-specific numerical recommendation into a universal number of successes required for mastery. Record variation, support, and quality criteria, distinguishing formative feedback from consequential certification. Without observing real performance, AI cannot claim to have verified professional competence.

**Original theoretical commitment:** Chapter 15 assumes that successful whole-task performance meeting all associated standards implies mastery of the underlying knowledge and routines; on that assumption, integral assessment is sufficient (PDF337, printed p322). Merely finishing a task is not this criterion.

**Cross-source design choice:** in combination with Marton and Booth, an observed completion may still leave an educationally important relationship unnoticed, particularly if the task, sampling, or standards do not test it. A meaningful variation may help investigate it; that is cross-source design, not an additional original component of 4C/ID.

## Worked Example

**Two distinct book cases:** Chapter 4 (PDF105, printed p90) first describes performance constraints on a conventional video-production task, including approval before moving between production stages. Separately, its completion-task example supplies incomplete videos, scripts, or storyboards for learners to finish, with tutor assistance in studying the partial materials. Support can vary while preserving the meaning of the whole task. This reference does not reproduce the complete video-production curriculum.

**Unvalidated skill adaptation:** to teach review of an interactive learning page, first demonstrate a complete annotated work product and show how source material supports arrows, calculations, and qualifications. Next provide an incomplete product to revise; then vary the source conditions and let the learner choose a presentation. If the user only needs one arrow fixed, fix it and explain rather than launch a course. Consider short drills only for repeated checks that actually require fluency.

## Key Takeaways

This systematic design framework primarily addresses complex learning. Simple factual questions and explicit single-step needs do not require curriculum reconstruction. Framework-level support does not validate a particular AI conversation. Chapter 16’s AI discussion is at PDF366–368 (printed pp351–353). It includes a case partly generated by ChatGPT 4.0 and reviewed by a pulmonologist as adequate but improvable; it also reports an AI-tutor study and proposes further design applications. The authors retain a reserved stance, require subject and design expertise, and warn about plausible errors. Neither these cases nor the proposed applications establish this skill’s efficacy or superiority to teachers.
