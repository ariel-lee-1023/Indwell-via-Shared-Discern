# A Dialogic Teaching Companion: responses that advance understanding

**Sections**: 9 | **Depth**: study
Robin Alexander, 2020. Based on the supplied Markdown and original PDF. L denotes original Markdown lines; chapter attribution is retained. Transferring classroom dialogue and professional-development research to one-to-one AI teaching is an unvalidated design proposal.

## Mental Model

**Author's position:** dialogic teaching has direction and epistemic demands while allowing learners' responses to change its course. Teacher and student talk, explicit explanation and inquiry, individual and collective activity can work together. Dialogue does not simply mean more student speech, no teacher explanation, or questions without answers. Locations: Chapter 7, L1538–1604 and L1620–1670; Chapter 5, L880–900, on oracy and pedagogy.

## Frameworks & Structure

The nine chapters are Prologue; Talk for learning; Versions of dialogue; Dialogue in other words; Grand dichotomy; Ingredient x; Frameworks and fundamentals; Professional development; Epilogue. The first six examine talk, versions of dialogue, instructional dichotomies, research, and practice. Chapter 7 develops the framework and repertoires; Chapter 8 addresses professional development. Its six constitutive segments are definitions, stance, justifications, principles, repertoires, and indicators; three explanatory sections precede them, making nine sections in the chapter framework (PDF137–138, printed pp126–127; L1500–1525). It concerns social relations, learner voice, and public life as well as cognition and instruction; it cannot be reduced to question stems.

### Six mutually constraining principles

This edition has six principles; do not import an earlier five-principle list. The following paraphrases Chapter 7, L1538–1604.

| Principle | What to observe | Conditional adaptation for AI teaching |
|---|---|---|
| Collective | Whether participants address a problem together and can engage with the material | Establish a mutually identifiable example, work product, or result; an AI monologue is not joint construction |
| Supportive | Whether tentative ideas, uncertainty, and mistakes can be expressed without humiliation | Give feedback on judgments, permit correction and declining to answer; support is not unconditional praise |
| Reciprocal | Whether participants listen to, consider, and respond to one another | Address the learner's latest relationship, evidence, or question in the next move |
| Deliberative | Whether reasons, evidence, and alternative accounts are examined | Give grounds for testing disagreement and acknowledge valid counterexamples; do not accept every claim equally |
| Cumulative | Whether contributions connect and advance coherent understanding | Retain established relationships and explain how a new move extends or revises them |
| Purposeful | Whether dialogue has an educational purpose and appropriate guidance | Choose explanation, demonstration, inquiry, or an attempt for the present purpose, not to prolong conversation |

Alexander's cumulative principle is not uncritical accumulation. His discussion of Mercer's categories of talk must not be collapsed into his own use of the same word. The principles are not boxes to tick in every turn.

## Decision Rules & Judgment

**Attribution boundary:** source concepts and reported findings are paraphrased, not quotations. Recommendations addressed to this AI skill, hypothetical learner signals, accessibility safeguards, and delivery choices are proposed designs unless explicitly identified as a book method or case. They are not independently validated effects.


Chapter 7 groups eight repertoires under culture and organization, patterns of exchange, and transaction to move (PDF147, printed p136). They address interactive culture, organizational settings, learning talk, teaching talk, questioning, extending, discussing, and arguing. Within the teaching-talk repertoire, rote, recitation, instruction, exposition, discussion, deliberation, argumentation, and dialogue are distinct options (PDF156, printed p145; L2921–2931). Select and coordinate them by purpose rather than declaring one permanently wrong.

**Uptake matters, not only whether a question is open.** A teacher's third turn can merely judge and close an exchange, or invite clarification, grounds, comparison, and revision. A checking question with a known answer can be useful; an irrelevant open question can fail to advance understanding. Locations: L1820–1860, L1920–1958.

Chapter 7 draws on Michaels and O'Connor (PDF162–164, printed pp151–153). Their four goals are to help learners share, expand, and clarify their own thinking; listen carefully to others; deepen their reasoning; and think with others. The nine moves, grouped under those goals (3, 1, 2, and 3 respectively), are: think time; say more; revoice with confirmation; rephrase another contribution; ask for evidence or reasoning; challenge or counterexample; agree/disagree and explain why; add on; explain another person's meaning. Preserve their attribution. These serve dialogic purposes rather than prescribing a sequence of questions. AI should not invent a second learner to perform the user's contribution to joint reasoning.

| Observed situation | Possible move | Signal to adjust |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning is unclear and immediate rebuttal may miss the issue | Offer a tentative paraphrase and invite correction; clarify only what changes the next move | A correction must change the initial interpretation |
| A conclusion lacks evidence | Point to a testable part of the material; demonstrate finding grounds when needed | Supply unfamiliar background or terminology instead of repeatedly asking why |
| Two explanations conflict substantively | Preserve their reasons and identify discriminating material or conditions | Revise when a counterexample succeeds; authority cannot override evidence |
| Exchanges become “right, good, next” | Take up the relationship in the response for comparison, explanation, or application | End or advance when the current purpose is met rather than forcing discussion |
| The user wants a direct explanation or declines practice | Explain clearly and leave room for questions | Unobserved independent performance remains unknown, not failed |

This table is the skill's design, not a validated AI procedure from the book.

## Worked Example

**Original skill example:** a learner says that passing a format check makes a summary trustworthy. Point to the two checks in the material: file format and access permission, neither of which verifies the summary's factual content. If the learner replies, “I know that; I am asking whether human review is just a formality,” the next move should address the review's grounds, what it can detect, and remaining uncertainty. Repeating the format explanation would miss the response.

If the learner objects that their system checks content before generation, acknowledge that the explanation depends on the supplied workflow and check for a separate verification stage. Reciprocity and purpose do not require AI always to concede, but they do require adjudication through the material.

## Social context and evidence limits

The book connects a supportive environment, participation, epistemic responsibility, and voice. Chapter 7 discusses social and epistemic norms, including Resnick, Michaels, and O’Connor’s accountability to the learning community, reasoning, and knowledge (PDF167, printed p156; L1990–2030), learner-initiated talk, and conditions requiring modelling (L2090–2120). Silence is not proof of absent thought, and speech frequency alone is not a quality measure.

Chapter 4 examines dialogue, argument, and deliberation in other fields. Drawing on Walton, it distinguishes inquiry, negotiation, information-seeking, deliberation, and related purposes from eristic argument aimed at winning (L750–775). The relevant standard concerns reasons and shared facts that withstand examination; not every exchange should become a debate. This reference does not expand the political or philosophical material.

Professional development and trials involve teachers, classroom organization, reflection, and sustained implementation. Frameworks change across projects: the book's current development arrangements must not be represented as identical to an earlier trial intervention (L2300–2320). Chapter 8 emphasizes reflection on actual classroom evidence rather than slogans (L2640–2670). This reference does not report trial effect sizes not checked individually, or pool differently defined dialogic interventions into one effectiveness estimate.

One-to-one AI lacks an actual peer group, classroom institutions, and a teacher's sustained knowledge of learners. Retain responsive adjustment, contestable reasons, and discernible purposes without simulating nonexistent group evidence or claiming equivalent classroom effects.

## Key Takeaways

Alexander protects voice, deliberation, and cumulative progress; 4C/ID specifies tasks and support more explicitly. Structure need not suppress dialogue, and dialogue need not become unguided discovery. With unfamiliar material, demonstration can establish a shared basis for discussion. With substantive disagreement, a predetermined answer should not close inquiry prematurely. These are cross-source design judgments, not evidence that the theories are identical.
